*Dots and Lines* is what this book was originally published as, which is a fitting name given the content it covers. Other than the beautiful cover image on my copy, this book covers a fun introduction to pure mathematics in the context of graph theory. 

Graph theory is the mathematical study of structures called 'graphs', a collection of dots (nodes) connected by lines (edges), and they're really quite neat. They see most of their applications in computer science, and are integrally part of combinatorics, but actually originated with a topological idea called 'The Seven Bridges of Königsberg".

They can be beautiful structures to just draw out and look at (see a few of my favourites: \(K_5\), the icosahedron graph, the Petersen graph, the Tutte–Coxeter graph), and I think that is what I found most appealing about them initially. This book does a great job at introducing them, along with the idea of mathematical rigour . 

It's slightly outdated, written before the four-colour theorem was proven, but there's an afterword in my copy explaining the proof (which is super interesting, being the first major proof-by-computer), but I don't think this takes away from what the book does right. My fear with an algorithmic approach to graph theory is that it can take away from the fact that it's really pretty, and the book lightly touching upon topology in one of the later chapters makes it really interesting.

In terms of complexity, I'd reccomend it for a wide audience. It's designed to be an easier mathematics book to read and not really written for mathematicians. Anybody interested in pure mathematics should be able to pick it up and read it. I personally read it in Year 12 (So, penultimate year of high school) and I think a younger reader could definitely get through it if they were curious (and if they are curious, I absolutely recommend they should try - reading maths books is something I wish I started a bit earlier). None of the proofs are particularly complex (the idea of mathematical proof is one you have to get yourself to agree with, but I've seen significantly more complex proofs from just the first few weeks of my undergraduate degree, so give it a go!).

Overall: 4.5/5 stars, would highly recommend for anyone looking to be curious about maths.
